How the numbers work

What every figure on the site means, what it leaves out, and where it comes from.

Pay

All pay is take-home pay. Figures cover the 13 western states: Alaska, Arizona, California, Colorado, Hawaii, Idaho, Montana, Nevada, New Mexico, Oregon, Utah, Washington and Wyoming. Each starts from the state's median salary for the role (BLS 2025) and subtracts 2026 federal income tax, Social Security, Medicare and state income tax for a single filer taking the standard deduction.

It leaves out city and county taxes, state disability and paid-leave payroll deductions (about 1% in California, Oregon and Washington), retirement contributions and health premiums, so real paychecks will be somewhat smaller. Half of workers in a state earn more than its median and half earn less, and new graduates usually start below it.

BLS doesn't split RNs by specialty, so specialty notes describe where a role usually falls, not a measured figure. Job growth figures are BLS national projections for 2025–35.

Cost of living and value

The "most take-home after cost of living" ranking in each job's details uses the Bureau of Economic Analysis' 2024 Regional Price Parities (released February 2026), where an average US state is 100 (California 110.7, New Mexico 92.2). It's a statewide average; Denver costs more than Pueblo.

The "raise covers school" figure divides the program cost by the after-tax raise over the job you'd have without the degree (nursing assistant pay for first nursing degrees, RN pay for graduate degrees), using US medians.

School cost and time

Costs on the career map are tuition and required fees for the whole program, not living expenses. Most are estimates built from published per-credit rates and typical public-school ranges, and are labeled that way.

Times are typical full-time ranges and vary by school and state. Certifications usually require a set number of practice hours in the specialty before you can sit for the exam.

Practical details

Schedule tags describe what's typical and vary a lot by employer. Experience requirements and competitiveness come from accreditors and program admissions pages; individual schools can ask for more.

Licensure, nurse practitioner practice authority and CRNA supervision rules are as of late 2026 and change through state legislation, so confirm with the state board before relying on them. Nurse practitioner scope depends on state law: Arizona and Colorado give NPs full practice authority, while many states require physician collaboration. Aid programs have limited funding, yearly deadlines and work commitments.

Schools and programs

The schools page lists real programs from each state board of nursing's official list of approved programs. Every fact comes from the school's own website and shows its source and the date it was last checked; anything that couldn't be confirmed says so instead of guessing.

NCLEX-RN pass rates are first-time rates from the state board where it publishes them (otherwise the school's own published figure, labeled as such). School-wide yearly tuition comes from the U.S. Department of Education's College Scorecard and is labeled "school-wide": nursing programs often add fees. Private for-profit schools and programs that are closing or paused aren't listed.

Spotted something wrong? Every program card has a "Report an error" link.
